# Circuits — the sacred loop

Electricity has one non-negotiable demand: **the path must be a complete
loop**. Charge flows out of a source, through your device, and *all the way
back*. Break the loop anywhere — anywhere at all — and everything stops
instantly.

This single fact explains more everyday mysteries than any other in this
course.

## The loop rule in the wild

- **A battery has two ends** because charge must leave one terminal and
  return to the other. One-ended batteries would be as useful as one-ended
  hoses.
- **A switch** is just a tiny drawbridge. "Off" means a few millimetres of
  air interrupt the loop — and air, being a stubborn insulator, ends the
  party. Your light switch doesn't "cut the power"; it opens a gap.
- **Birds sit on power lines unbothered** because they touch only one wire —
  no loop through the bird, no current through the bird. (A bird stretching
  wing-to-wing between *two* lines would have a very bad day.)
- **Plugs have at least two pins** — one lane out, one lane home. The third
  pin is a bodyguard; his story comes in the safety chapter.

## Series vs parallel: the two ways to wire anything

Connect two bulbs to one battery and you have a choice:

**In series** — one after another on a single loop. The same current threads
through everything, like beads on one string. Downside: one bulb dies, the
loop breaks, *everything* goes dark, and you spend Christmas Eve testing
forty identical little bulbs to find the traitor. (Old fairy lights. Never
forget.)

**In parallel** — each bulb gets its own private loop back to the source.
Everyone receives full voltage, and one failure changes nothing for the
others. Your entire home is wired in parallel, which is why the microwave
dying doesn't take the fridge with it.

> **⚡ Spark fact:** Modern LED fairy lights are still wired in series — it's
> cheaper — but each LED contains a microscopic bypass that closes the loop
> if it dies. The engineers didn't fix the tradition; they gave every bulb
> its own tiny understudy.

## The short circuit: the loop gone rogue

Normally, current is forced through something useful — a bulb, a motor — that
resists the flow and soaks up the energy as work. A **short circuit** is
what happens when charge finds a shortcut with almost no resistance: say, the
two wires in a frayed cable touching.

Remember Ohm's law: `current = voltage ÷ resistance`. Push 230 volts through
nearly-zero resistance and the current goes *enormous*, instantly. The wire
becomes a heater with no off switch — melting insulation, and, historically,
starting a memorable number of fires.

This is why every circuit in your home has a chaperone — a fuse or breaker —
whose entire job is noticing "that's too many amps" and breaking the loop
faster than trouble can spread. We'll meet them properly behind your walls.

## One more loop trick: doing work along the way

Why does forcing current through things make heat and motion? Because the
electron crowd bumps and shoves its way through the material's atoms, handing
over energy with every collision. Concentrate that shoving in a thin
high-resistance wire and it glows — congratulations, you've invented the
toaster, the kettle, the old lightbulb and the sauna heater, which are all
*literally the same appliance* in different costumes.

So: we have a push, a loop, and work being done. Time to zoom out and ask the
big question — where does the push come from? Prepare to meet an unreasonable
number of spinning magnets.
