# The grid — the biggest machine ever built

Between the spinning magnet and your phone charger lies the **grid**: every
power line, pylon, transformer and substation, all physically connected into
one colossal circuit. Engineers half-jokingly call it the largest machine
humans have ever built, and they're not really joking. Most of Europe is
*one electrically connected object*.

## The problem: wires charge a toll

Send current through a long wire and resistance taxes you the whole way,
bleeding energy off as heat. Remember the chapter-two rule: the heat scales
with the *current*. Double the amps, quadruple the loss. Long-distance power
delivery at household voltage would cook its own cables and arrive
embarrassingly thin.

The escape hatch is the power equation: `watts = volts × amps`. The same
power can travel as **huge voltage and tiny current** instead. Slash the
amps a hundredfold and the heat-loss problem shrinks ten-thousand-fold.

So that's exactly what the grid does:

1. **Step UP** — right at the power station, transformers crank the voltage
   to as much as **400,000 volts**.
2. **Travel** — power crosses the country on those big steel pylons, losing
   only a few percent.
3. **Step DOWN** — a chain of substations and neighbourhood transformers
   lowers it in stages, until the cable entering your home carries a polite
   **230 volts**.

## The transformer: the grid's quiet genius

The device doing all this stepping is the **transformer** — two coils of
wire sharing an iron core, nothing moving. AC in one coil creates a rippling
magnetic field; the field induces voltage in the other coil; and the ratio of
coil windings sets the ratio of voltages. More turns, more volts. It's
Faraday's magnet trick again, running on the *magnet* side this time.

This is the device that won the AC-versus-DC war. It's also that humming
metal cylinder on a pole, and the reason your phone charger has a little
warm brick in it — that brick's heart is a transformer the size of a sugar
cube.

## The balancing act nobody notices

Here's the grid's wildest property: **electricity is barely stored anywhere
in it**. At every instant, the amount being generated must equal the amount
being used — across the whole continent, in real time, forever.

When a million kettles switch on at half-time, generation must rise *within
seconds* to match. The grid's heartbeat gives it away: too much demand and
every generator on the continent physically slows, dragging the frequency
below 50 Hz; too much supply and it creeps above. Control rooms watch that
number like an ICU monitor, ordering plants up and down around the clock —
hydro dams are beloved here, because a sluice gate opens in seconds.

> **⚡ Spark fact:** Sweden is a long country with a plumbing problem: most
> power is made in the river-rich north, while most people live in the
> south. The wires between them can only carry so much — so Sweden is split
> into four electricity price areas, SE1 to SE4, and the further south you
> live, the more that same northern kilowatt-hour tends to cost. Geography,
> visible on your power bill.

## When the dance fails

The grid's interconnectedness is its strength and its horror-movie premise.
A major line fails; its load instantly shoves onto neighbouring lines; one
of *those* overloads and trips; and a domino wave can black out regions in
minutes. Big blackouts are rare precisely because grid operators are
professionally paranoid — running drills, keeping reserves spinning, and
splitting the network before a fault can cascade.

From here, the story turns domestic. That 230-volt cable has reached your
building — time to follow it through the wall and meet the small metal box
that runs your home's private grid.
