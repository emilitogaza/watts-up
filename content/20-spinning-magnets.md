# Spinning magnets — where power comes from

Here is the best-kept open secret in energy: strip away the cooling towers,
the dams, the turbine blades and the political arguments, and nearly every
power station on Earth is **the same machine** — something making a magnet
spin next to a coil of wire.

## The trick: induction

In 1831, Michael Faraday discovered that moving a magnet past a wire *pushes
the electrons* in that wire. No touching, no fuel, no ceremony — motion plus
magnetism equals voltage. It felt like a magic trick then and honestly it
still does.

Spin the magnet continuously and you push the electron crowd continuously:
congratulations, you've built a **generator**. Essentially every watt you've
ever used was made this way.

## Same machine, different ways to spin it

All the famous power-plant types are really just answers to one question:
*"What shall we use to spin the magnet?"*

| Power plant | What spins the magnet |
| ----------- | --------------------- |
| Coal / gas  | Burn stuff → boil water → steam blasts through a turbine |
| **Nuclear** | Split atoms → boil water → steam blasts through a turbine (yes, a nuclear plant is a very fancy kettle) |
| **Hydro**   | A river, falling through a dam, shoves the turbine directly |
| **Wind**    | The wind pushes giant blades — a windmill with better PR |
| Geothermal  | The Earth's own heat boils the water for you |

The one mainstream exception: **solar panels**. No spinning, no turbine —
sunlight knocks electrons loose in a silicon sandwich directly. Solar is the
only major power source that makes electricity without anything moving,
which is genuinely strange company at the power-plant family reunion.

> **⚡ Spark fact:** In Sweden, the magnets are mostly spun by water and
> atoms — roughly 40% hydro (rivers up north doing heavy lifting), 30%
> nuclear, and a fast-growing slice of wind. Burning things for electricity
> barely features, which is why Swedish power is among the cleanest in
> Europe.

## Why AC — and what "50 Hz" means

A spinning magnet doesn't push electrons steadily in one direction. As its
poles sweep past the coil — north, south, north, south — the push reverses
with every half-turn. The current in the wire surges forward, then backward,
in a smooth wave: **alternating current (AC)**.

Grids run at a fixed rhythm: **50 cycles per second (50 Hz)** in Europe,
60 Hz in North America. That number isn't a setting in a menu — it's the
*actual rotation speed* of the physical generators. Every turbine on the
European grid spins in lockstep, like an enormous synchronised dance troupe,
and the frequency on your wall outlet is their live heartbeat.

Batteries, meanwhile, produce **direct current (DC)** — a steady one-way
push. Your electronics secretly all want DC, which is why every charger
contains a little converter, and why it gets warm doing it.

## Why AC won

In the 1880s, Thomas Edison (team DC) and George Westinghouse with Nikola
Tesla (team AC) fought an actual commercial war over which current would
power the world. AC won for one killer reason: **its voltage can be
transformed** — stepped up enormously high for efficient long-distance
travel, then back down for safe home use — using a device with no moving
parts at all.

That device, and the continent-sized machine built around it, is the next
