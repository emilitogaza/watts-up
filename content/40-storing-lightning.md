# Storing lightning

The grid chapter revealed electricity's awkward secret: it's made and used
in the same instant, like live theatre. But your phone works on a plane and
your flashlight works in a blackout — so clearly *somebody* figured out how
to bottle the stuff. Enter the battery: possibly the most underrated object
you own.

## How a battery actually works

A battery doesn't store electricity — it stores a **chemical argument**.

Inside are two different materials (electrodes) that would *love* to swap
electrons, separated by a substance (the electrolyte) that refuses to let
electrons pass directly. The electrons' only legal route from one side to
the other is *outside the battery* — through your device.

Plug in a bulb and the deal is struck: electrons stream out of one terminal,
through the filament (doing work on the way), and home to the other side.
When the chemical argument is settled, the battery is dead.

**Recharging** runs the argument in reverse: your charger pushes electrons
back the unpopular direction, re-stressing the chemistry so it can argue
again. Rechargeable chemistries survive thousands of these round trips.

## The lithium era

Lithium-ion batteries earned their monopoly on modern life by being light
(lithium is the third-lightest element), energetic and endlessly
rechargeable — the reason a phone lasts a day and an electric car crosses
counties. Their quirks follow from the chemistry too: they hate freezing
cold (the internal chemistry turns sluggish — your phone dying on a ski
slope is it going numb, not broken), hate being *fully* drained, and ideally
loaf around the middle of their charge range like sensible retirees.

## Storing electricity by the mountain

Phones are solved. The *grid* is not — a wind-still winter evening needs
somewhere to pull terawatt-hours from, and battery farms that size remain
expensive. The heavyweight solution is delightfully medieval:

**Pumped hydro.** When power is cheap and plentiful, pump water up a
mountain into a reservoir. When power is scarce, let it fall back down
through turbines. The mountain *is* the battery — round-trip efficiency
around 75–80%, capacity measured in entire cities, no exotic chemistry
required. The vast majority of the world's stored electricity sits in
elevated lakes right now, waiting.

The supporting cast is growing fast: grid-scale battery parks (built for
seconds-fast frequency rescue — remember the 50 Hz heartbeat), heat stored
in sand or molten salt, flywheels, and **green hydrogen** — using surplus
electricity to split water, keeping the hydrogen as fuel or industrial
feedstock. Northern Sweden's fossil-free steel projects are betting big on
exactly that trick.

> **⚡ Spark fact:** A Tesla's battery pack stores about 75 kWh — the energy
> in roughly *seven litres of petrol*. That petrol fits in a lunch bag; the
> battery weighs half a tonne. It's an unfair fight that EVs win anyway,
> because an electric motor turns ~90% of its energy into motion while a
> petrol engine manages ~25%, spending the rest impersonating a radiator.

## The bigger picture

Storage is the missing puzzle piece of the clean-energy era. Sun and wind
are now often the *cheapest* electricity ever produced — but they keep
farmer's hours, not kettle hours. Every improvement in storage lets more of
the grid run on weather instead of fire: batteries for the minutes, water
for the nights, hydrogen (maybe) for the winters.

The spinning magnets aren't going anywhere — but the grid your grandkids
inherit will likely be a stranger, smarter machine: millions of solar roofs,
car batteries selling power *back* during peak hours, and software
conducting the whole orchestra.

That closes the first arc — the big world of power. Now the course changes
scale entirely: down from the continent-sized machine to the electron-sized
one, onto the workbench where circuits are measured, understood, and
occasionally mourned.
