# What electricity actually is

Ask ten people what electricity is and nine will say "electrons flowing
through wires, really fast." It's a lovely image. It's also mostly wrong, in
ways that make the truth much more interesting.

## Start with the atom

Everything is made of atoms, and atoms come with charged particles:
**protons** (positive) locked in the core, and **electrons** (negative)
buzzing around the outside. Opposite charges attract, like charges repel —
that's the entire rulebook. Every spark, every lightning bolt, every phone
charge is that one rule playing out at scale.

In most materials, electrons are homebodies — stuck firmly to their atoms.
But in **metals**, the outermost electrons are barely attached at all. They
drift around freely between atoms, like a crowd milling about a plaza. This
"sea of loose electrons" is why metals conduct electricity and rubber
doesn't. A copper wire is essentially a pipe pre-filled with a electron
crowd, waiting for instructions.

## The great speed myth

Here's the fun part. When you flip a light switch, the light turns on
instantly — so the electrons must be moving near light speed, right?

Nope. In a typical wire, the electrons themselves shuffle along at less than
**a millimetre per second**. A snail would comfortably overtake them. An
electron leaving your wall socket right now would take *hours* to reach your
phone.

So why is the light instant? Because what travels fast isn't the electrons —
it's the **push**. The electric field propagates through the wire at close to
the speed of light, setting the entire electron crowd in motion at once.

Think of a garden hose already full of water: open the tap and water comes
out the far end *immediately* — not because the new water crossed the hose,
but because the hose was already full and the pressure arrived instantly.
The wire is the hose. The electrons are the water. The field is the pressure.

> **⚡ Spark fact:** In your wall outlet, the electrons don't even *go*
> anywhere. Household power is **alternating current** — the crowd shuffles
> back and forth 50 times a second (more on that later), netting zero
> distance. The electrons that power your toaster tonight are the same ones
> that were in the cable when it left the factory. You're not buying
> electrons; you're buying *the push*.

## So what is "electricity"?

When we say electricity, we usually mean **electric current**: a coordinated
drift of charge through a conductor. To get a current you need exactly two
things:

1. **A push** — a difference in electrical pressure between two points
   (that's *voltage*, next chapter's star).
2. **A path** — an unbroken loop of conductive material for the charge to
   move through (that's a *circuit*, the chapter after).

Give the electron crowd a push and a path, and it does work along the way —
heating a filament, spinning a motor, flipping the tiny switches in your
laptop billions of times per second.

## Static: electricity's feral cousin

Before wires, humans knew electricity mainly as the crackly sting of static —
rub wool on amber and it snaps at you. Static is what happens when charge
*piles up* with nowhere to go: you shuffle across a carpet, scrape electrons
off it, and become a slightly overcharged human until — *zap* — a doorknob
offers a path and the surplus jumps.

A lightning bolt is exactly the same event with the volume turned all the way
up: a cloud spends a storm charging itself like a giant carpet-shuffling sock,
until the difference gets so large that *the air itself* is forced to become
a path.

Same physics, different scale: the doorknob zap is a few thousand volts and
utterly harmless; lightning is a few hundred *million* volts and very much
not. Which raises the obvious question — what exactly is a volt? Next
chapter.
