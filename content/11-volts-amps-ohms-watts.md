# Volts, amps, ohms & watts

Every charger, bulb and appliance you own is covered in these four words, and
they're all describing one simple story. The classic way to tell it — used by
every electricity teacher since forever, because it *works* — is to imagine
water in pipes.

## The water analogy

Picture a water tower feeding a garden hose:

| Electrical thing | Water version | What it means |
| ---------------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| **Voltage** (volts, V) | Water *pressure* | How hard the charge is being pushed |
| **Current** (amps, A) | Flow *rate* | How much charge actually flows per second |
| **Resistance** (ohms, Ω) | Pipe *narrowness* | How much the path fights the flow |
| **Power** (watts, W) | Pressure × flow | How much work gets done per second |

- A tall water tower = high pressure = **high voltage**. Nothing needs to be
  flowing yet — a battery on a shelf holds its voltage like a full tower
  holds its pressure, patiently.
- Open the tap and water moves. The amount flowing per second = **current**.
- Pinch the hose and less flows despite the same pressure. That pinch is
  **resistance** — and pinching makes the hose warm. (Remember that: it's
  how toasters work.)

## The one equation worth memorising

These three are locked together by **Ohm's law**:

```
current = voltage ÷ resistance        (I = V / R)
```

More push → more flow. More resistance → less flow. That's it — that's the
law. A huge share of all electrical engineering is this one line wearing
increasingly elaborate hats.

## Watts: where the action is

**Power** measures how fast energy is being delivered, and it's beautifully
simple:

```
watts = volts × amps                  (P = V × I)
```

Pressure times flow. A device that sips 1 amp from a 230-volt outlet is using
230 watts. Your phone charger nibbles ~20 W, a kettle gulps ~2,000 W, and an
electric car charger inhales ~11,000 W. Same outlet voltage — wildly
different currents.

## And the thing you actually pay for: kWh

Your electricity bill doesn't charge for watts — it charges for watts *over
time*. A **kilowatt-hour (kWh)** is 1,000 watts running for one hour. It's
the "litres of water used" on the bill, where watts were "how hard the tap
was open."

- Kettle: 2,000 W × 3 minutes = **0.1 kWh** per tea.
- Fridge: ~100 W around the clock ≈ **2.4 kWh** a day — the quiet marathon
  runner of your home.
- One kWh costs roughly what a piece of candy does. The magic is *cheap*.

> **⚡ Spark fact:** "Volt", "amp", "ohm" and "watt" are all surnames —
> Alessandro **Volta** (battery inventor), André-Marie **Ampère**, Georg
> **Ohm**, and James **Watt** of steam-engine fame. Getting your name
> lowercased into a unit is the closest thing physics has to sainthood.

## Why this matters practically

This one chapter quietly explains half of everyday electrical life:

- **Why thick cables for ovens and thin ones for lamps?** More current needs
  a wider "pipe" — a thin wire carrying a big current is a pinched hose, and
  it gets *hot*.
- **Why 230 V at home but 400,000 V on pylons?** Power = volts × amps, so
  raising the volts lets you slash the amps for the same power — and fewer
  amps means less heat wasted in the wires. (The grid chapter cashes in on
  this.)
- **Why does the fuse blow when the kettle, heater and toaster run at once?**
  Each adds current; the fuse is a bouncer counting total amps. Which brings
  us to circuits — the loop all of this flows around. Next up.
