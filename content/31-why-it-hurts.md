# Why it hurts — electricity & the body

Time for the serious chapter — served straight, because understanding this
properly is genuinely useful. Electricity injures thousands of people a year,
and almost every incident involves someone who *thought* they knew the rules.

## Your body is part of the circuit

Everything so far applies to you directly: you conduct electricity (nerves
literally run on electrical signals), and if you bridge two points at
different voltages, current takes the loop *through you*. How bad that is
depends on three things:

1. **How much current flows** — the real villain.
2. **The path it takes** — hand-to-hand or hand-to-foot crosses the heart;
   finger-to-thumb on one hand mostly doesn't.
3. **How long it lasts** — which is why the RCD's speed matters so much.

## "Volts don't kill, amps do" — the truth in the cliché

The famous saying is *half* right. Damage is done by current: at around
**0.001 amps** you feel a tingle; at ~0.01 amps muscles clamp shut — you may
be unable to let go; near **0.05–0.1 amps** crossing the chest, the heart's
own electrical rhythm can be knocked into chaos. A tenth of what a lamp
draws.

But don't let the cliché make you dismiss voltage — because Ohm's law sets
the current: `amps = volts ÷ your resistance`. Volts are the *push*; without
enough push, no dangerous current flows through skin. That's why a 1.5 V
battery is harmless and 230 V is not. The full sentence is: **volts decide
whether a dangerous current can flow; amps decide what it does to you.**

Your resistance is the wildcard. Dry skin resists decently; **wet skin
resists dramatically less** — same outlet, far more current. Electricity and
bathrooms have a whole legal code governing their relationship, and this is
why.

## The myths, retired

- **"Rubber shoes make me safe."** Consumer soles are thin, often carbon-laced,
  frequently damp — a speed bump, not armour. Also irrelevant if you touch
  live and neutral with two hands and the loop never visits your feet.
- **"Household voltage is basically harmless."** 230 V across a damp
  hand-to-hand path can exceed the fibrillation threshold with room to
  spare. Most electrical fatalities involve ordinary household voltage —
  partly because familiarity breeds one-more-shortcut.
- **"If it was dangerous, I'd have felt it before."** The same outlet can
  tingle one day and kill another; contact quality, moisture and path change
  everything. Previous survival is not a safety certificate.
- **"Birds on wires prove it's fine to touch one wire."** The bird isn't
  earthed. You, on a ladder, are. Your feet complete a loop the bird simply
  doesn't have.
- **"DC is safe, AC is the bad one."** Both are dangerous in different
  flavours — AC's 50 Hz is unluckily good at triggering muscle lock and
  heart chaos, while high-voltage DC burns and won't let the arc go.

## Lightning: the grid's wild sibling

A bolt carries tens of thousands of amps for a fraction of a second. The
counterintuitive survival rules follow from what you now know: crouching low
beats lying flat (ground current flows *along* the surface — less
between-your-contact-points voltage with feet together), a car is safe not
because of the tyres but because the metal shell routes current around the
squishy interior, and open-field solitude during a storm is a hobby to
schedule for another day.

> **⚡ Spark fact:** The medical plot twist — the same current that can stop
> a heart can restart one. A defibrillator is a precisely dosed, precisely
> timed electric shock that momentarily silences every heart cell at once so
> the natural pacemaker can restore order. The difference between the
> villain and the hero is *control*.

## The actual safety rules, condensed

Respect beats fear: turn the circuit off at the panel before working on
anything, verify with a tester rather than trust, keep water out of the
story, use RCD-protected outlets outdoors and in bathrooms, replace frayed
cables instead of taping them, and treat every wire as live until proven
otherwise. That's 95% of electrical safety in one sentence each.
