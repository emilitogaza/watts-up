# Behind your walls

Somewhere in your home — a hallway cupboard, the laundry room, a spooky
corner of the basement — hangs a grey box that runs your personal grid.
Let's decode it, and finally answer what all those switches and pins
actually do.

## The fuse box (a.k.a. the panel)

The 230-volt feed enters your home and splits into separate **circuits** —
one for the kitchen counters, one for the oven, a few for lights and
outlets. Each circuit passes through its own guardian in the panel:

- **Old school: fuses** — a deliberate weak link. A thin wire inside melts
  ("blows") when the current exceeds its rating, breaking the loop. Effective
  and single-use, like an airbag.
- **Modern: circuit breakers** — spring-loaded switches that trip on excess
  current, using an electromagnet (instant, for short-circuit surges) and a
  heat-sensitive strip (slower, for gradual overloads). Reset with a flick —
  reusable airbags.

When the kettle + heater + toaster trifecta kills the kitchen: that's the
breaker doing its one job. It counted the amps; there were too many; it
opened the loop before the *wiring in your walls* became the toaster.

## Earth: the third pin's secret life

Look at a grounded plug: two pins carry the working loop — **live** (the
push) and **neutral** (the return lane). The third contact, **earth**,
carries nothing at all. Normally.

It's connected, through the panel, literally into the ground — and inside an
appliance it's bolted to the metal casing. Now imagine a wire shakes loose
inside your washing machine and touches the chassis. Without earth, the
casing silently becomes live, waiting for a damp human to complete the loop.
*With* earth, the stray current instantly floods down the earth wire — a
dead short — and the breaker slams the circuit off before you ever touch it.

The third pin is a bodyguard who does nothing for years, then takes the hit
for you in a millisecond.

## The RCD: the sharpest guardian of all

The breaker protects your *wiring*; it takes 10+ amps to impress it. A human
can be electrocuted by less than a tenth of one amp — the breaker wouldn't
even look up. Enter the **residual current device** (RCD, jordfelsbrytare,
GFCI — every country names it differently and every modern home has one).

Its logic is elegant: current out through live must equal current back
through neutral, *always*. If even 0.03 amps go missing — because they're
detouring through a person into the ground — the RCD notices the imbalance
and cuts the power in a few hundredths of a second. It's the difference
between a nasty jolt and a funeral, bolted into your hallway.

> **⚡ Spark fact:** That **TEST button** on your panel you've never pressed?
> It creates a tiny fake fault so the RCD can prove it still works. It's
> meant to be pressed a couple of times a year. Go on — press it. Best case,
> you've tested a life-safety device; worst case, you get to re-set some
> clocks.

## Why sockets look like that

Every design detail of a wall socket is a safety argument that somebody won:

- **Recessed sockets** (the little cave): the plug's pins are fully inside
  before they touch power — no half-inserted pins for stray fingers.
- **Shuttered holes**: internal doors that only open when both pins push
  together, defeating the classic toddler-with-a-fork experiment.
- **Polarised or earthed shapes** ensure the safety features line up the
  intended way, every time.

And the fat cables for the oven versus slim ones for lamps? Chapter three's
rule made physical: more amps need a wider pipe, and wall wiring is sized —
by law, with margins — for the worst day of its life, not the average one.

All these guardians exist because electricity, useful as it is, has zero
sense of humour about the human body. Exactly how it hurts us — and which
famous "facts" about that are nonsense — is next.
